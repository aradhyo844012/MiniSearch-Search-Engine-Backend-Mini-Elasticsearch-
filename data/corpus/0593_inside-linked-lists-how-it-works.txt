Inside Linked Lists: How It Works

This article walks through what happens inside Linked Lists, step by step. If you build software for long enough, you will run into Linked Lists. The term refers to chains of nodes where each node stores a value and a reference to the next node.

To understand Linked Lists, start with nodes. Then move on to head and tail pointers, and finally look at singly and doubly linked lists and pointer updates. A good way to remember nodes is to connect it with a concrete example from your own code.

Ask a teammate to explain singly and doubly linked lists back to you; teaching is a quick way to find gaps. Write a few tests that show how Linked Lists should behave. Tests double as documentation for your teammates. Start small by applying Linked Lists to one case, such as queues and stacks, before rolling it out more widely.

First, nodes sets the stage for everything else. Next, head and tail pointers builds on it. After that, singly and doubly linked lists comes into play, and finally pointer updates ties the pieces together. Seeing the sequence once, with a tiny example, makes the whole topic easier to remember.

Algorithms are easiest to learn by tracing small examples on paper before writing any code. Choosing the right data structure often matters more than clever tuning later.

If you are comfortable with Linked Lists, explore Binary Search next. The two often appear in the same project.

In short, careful study of Linked Lists pays off. Spend time on nodes and head and tail pointers, and the rest will follow.
