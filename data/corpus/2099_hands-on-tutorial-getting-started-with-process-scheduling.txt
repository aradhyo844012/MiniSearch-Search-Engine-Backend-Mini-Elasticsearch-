Hands-On Tutorial: Getting Started with Process Scheduling

This hands-on tutorial takes you from zero to a small working experiment with Process Scheduling. Many students first meet Process Scheduling in a classroom, but it matters just as much in production systems. The term refers to the way an operating system decides which process runs on the CPU and for how long.

Step 1: Install or open top and create a new empty project folder.
Step 2: Read about time slices and write down a definition in your own words.
Step 3: Build the smallest possible example that uses priorities.
Step 4: Add one test or print statement that proves it works.
Step 5: Extend the example to handle a case such as sharing CPUs among programs.
Step 6: Break your example on purpose and see what happens. A good experiment: starvation of low priority tasks.

Popular tools and libraries include top, htop, and perf.

Beginners often stumble on three things. First: starvation of low priority tasks. Second: too many context switches. Third: ignoring CPU affinity.

In short, careful study of Process Scheduling pays off. Spend time on time slices and priorities, and the rest will follow.
