A Short History of Database Sharding

Ideas behind Database Sharding developed gradually as engineers ran into practical limits and shared what they learned. Many students first meet Database Sharding in a classroom, but it matters just as much in production systems. The term refers to splitting a large dataset across several databases so each holds only part of it.

Most slow pages in data-driven applications trace back to a handful of slow queries. Data that must survive restarts and crashes belongs in a database rather than in memory.

Database Sharding and SQL Joins are closely related, so learning both gives a fuller picture of Databases.

Four ideas sit at the heart of Database Sharding: shard keys, routing, rebalancing, and cross-shard queries. Each one builds on the one before it. Sketching cross-shard queries on paper before coding helps many learners see how the pieces fit.

A solid grasp of Database Sharding also makes SQL Joins easier to understand.

In short, careful study of Database Sharding pays off. Spend time on shard keys and routing, and the rest will follow.
