Best Practices for TCP and UDP

The practices below come from patterns that tend to work well across many teams and projects. Many students first meet TCP and UDP in a classroom, but it matters just as much in production systems. The term refers to the two main transport protocols, one reliable and ordered and the other fast and connectionless.

1. Ask a teammate to explain ports back to you; teaching is a quick way to find gaps.
2. Automate repetitive steps so that results are easy to reproduce.
3. Keep an eye on this risk during code review: assuming UDP guarantees delivery.
4. Write a few tests that show how TCP and UDP should behave. Tests double as documentation for your teammates.
5. Measure before you optimize. Use Wireshark or a simple timer to see where time is actually spent.

In practice you can start with Wireshark, then explore netcat or tcpdump as your needs grow.

Network failures are normal, so well-designed software expects timeouts and retries.

A solid grasp of TCP and UDP also makes TLS Handshake easier to understand.

None of this needs special hardware or expensive software. A laptop, curiosity and a few small experiments are enough to get comfortable with TCP and UDP.
