A Beginner's Guide to Deadlocks

This guide is written for readers who are new to Deadlocks and want a clear starting point. The term Deadlocks refers to situations where two or more tasks wait for each other forever. This article explains the idea in plain language and points out where it fits in real projects.

Logs and system monitors are the first place to look when a machine misbehaves.

The vocabulary of Deadlocks is small. Learn mutual exclusion, hold and wait, circular wait, and lock ordering, and most documentation will start to make sense. Sketching lock ordering on paper before coding helps many learners see how the pieces fit.

People use Deadlocks for database lock conflicts, for multi-threaded programs, and for resource allocation.

In practice you can start with thread dumps, then explore database deadlock logs or static analyzers as your needs grow.

If you are comfortable with Deadlocks, explore Process Scheduling next. The two often appear in the same project.

Watch out for these problems: acquiring locks in inconsistent order; holding locks while waiting on I/O; and no timeouts on locks.

Read the official documentation for database deadlock logs; it often explains hold and wait better than blog posts do. Write a few tests that show how Deadlocks should behave. Tests double as documentation for your teammates.

Keep experimenting, keep notes, and revisit Deadlocks whenever a new project gives you a reason.
