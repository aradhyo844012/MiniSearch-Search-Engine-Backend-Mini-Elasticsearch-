The Trade-offs of Decision Trees

Every design choice has a cost, and Decision Trees is no exception. The term Decision Trees refers to models that make predictions by asking a sequence of simple questions about the data. This article explains the idea in plain language and points out where it fits in real projects.

On the positive side, Decision Trees is well suited to classifying customers and explaining predictions. On the other hand, it is not free. Keep these risks in mind: deep trees that overfit; ignoring class imbalance; unstable results from small data changes. Whether the trade is worth it depends on how much splits and entropy and Gini impurity matter to your project.

Beginners often stumble on three things. First: deep trees that overfit. Second: ignoring class imbalance. Third: unstable results from small data changes.

Ask a teammate to explain leaf nodes back to you; teaching is a quick way to find gaps. Keep an eye on this risk during code review: deep trees that overfit.

Reproducible experiments make it far easier to trust and improve a model.

None of this needs special hardware or expensive software. A laptop, curiosity and a few small experiments are enough to get comfortable with Decision Trees.
